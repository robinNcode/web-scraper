## Overview

It maintains that many Qur’anic verses are historically contingent on particular people, questions or events, and therefore the Qur’an’s “content” depends on human reality. By highlighting this dependence, the author concludes that calling Allah both wholly aseitic and the author of a human‑contingent text is logically inconsistent. ### Claim 3: If the Qur’an is an uncreated attribute, its human‑contingent content makes that attribute dependent on creation  
- **Statement:** “যদি কোরআন আল্লাহর অনাদি সিফাত হয়, আর সেই সিফাতের নির্দিষ্ট বিষয়বস্তু যদি মানুষের অস্তিত্ব, প্রশ্ন, অপরাধ, যুদ্ধ ও সামাজিক ঘটনার ওপর নির্ভরশীল হয়, তবে আল্লাহর একটি অনাদি সিফাত সৃষ্ট মানুষের ওপর নির্ভরশীল হয়ে পড়ে।”  
- **Type:** causal / interpretive  
- **Assessment:** partially supported (philosophically contestable)  
- **Critique:** The argument rests on the premise that an “attribute” is identical to the *specific content* of the Qur’an. Classical Islamic theology distinguishes between the *attribute* (the Qur’an as the eternal speech of God) and the *revelation* (the temporal, contextualized recitation). Scholars such as Al‑Ghazālī (*Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al‑dīn*, 11th c.) argue that the Qur’an’s *meaning* is uncreated, while its *letters* and *occasion‑specific rulings* are created. Hence, the dependence of particular verses on human events does not render the attribute itself dependent. The claim highlights a genuine philosophical tension, but it overlooks the nuanced theological distinction that many Muslim scholars already make.
